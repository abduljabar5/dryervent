Dryer Vents in a Minnesota Winter: Snow, Ice and Frozen Flappers

Minnesota is hard on dryer vents in ways that warm-climate advice never mentions. Here is what happens between November and March and how to get ahead of it.

Snow drifts block low wall vents

Basement laundry rooms vent 1–4 feet above grade, right at drift height. One good storm and the flapper is packed shut. The dryer then pushes exhaust back into the duct, and damp lint piles up fast. After storms, clear 12 inches around the cap, and keep the snowblower chute pointed away from it. For chronic drifting we install a vent extension that raises the outlet.

Frost and ice at the cap

Warm, humid exhaust hits a cap that is below zero and condenses. Light frost is normal. Thick ice that freezes the flapper shut or builds icicles means airflow is too slow to clear moisture before it freezes, which usually points to lint buildup upstream.

Cold caps make lint sticky

In summer, lint arrives at the cap dry and much of it blows out. In winter, moisture condenses in the last few feet of duct and lint packs into a damp felt. This is why fall is the best time to clean: you start winter with a clear run.

Cold air leaking in

A flapper stuck open by lint, or a broken damper, lets outside air flow backward down the duct into your laundry room. If the laundry room is noticeably colder than the rest of the basement, check the cap.

Roof vents in winter

We clean roof vents year-round. When the roof is icy we clean from inside with an extended rod system and inspect the cap by pole camera. If the cap needs hands-on clearing we return free when the roof is safe. More on roof vents.

Your pre-winter checklist

  • Book a cleaning in October or November
  • Replace any foil or vinyl transition hose
  • Trim shrubs back 12 inches from the cap
  • Mark a low cap with a stake so you can find it under snow
  • Check the flapper after every big storm

Related questions

How do I find my dryer vent outside?

Walk around the exterior of the house on the same side as your laundry room and look for a 4-inch hooded cap with a flap, usually 1–4 feet above the ground for basement laundry or at second-floor height for upstairs laundry. Run the dryer and look for the flap moving or feel for warm air.

My outdoor dryer vent is behind bushes. Is that a problem?

Yes if the bushes touch the cap. Shrubs trap lint, block the flapper, and in winter hold snow against the opening. Keep 12 inches of clearance so the flap swings freely.

Should my dryer vent have a screen on it?

No. Building code prohibits screens on dryer exhaust terminations because they trap lint. If your cap has a screen we remove it and recommend a louvered or pest-guard cap designed for dryers.

What is a dryer vent flapper and why does it matter?

The flapper is the hinged door on the exterior cap. It opens from airflow when the dryer runs and closes to keep cold air and pests out. A stuck or broken flapper either leaks cold air into your house or blocks exhaust.

Can snow block my dryer vent?

Yes. Drifting snow against a low wall vent blocks the flapper and forces exhaust back into the duct. After big snowfalls, clear 12 inches around the cap. We also install vent extensions that raise the outlet above typical drift height.

Why is cold air coming into my laundry room from the dryer?

The exterior flapper is stuck open or missing, letting outside air flow backward down the duct. We replace damaged caps so the flap seals when the dryer is off.
